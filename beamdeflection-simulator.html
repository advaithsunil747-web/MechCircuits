About the Beam Deflection Simulator

The Beam Deflection Simulator is an interactive engineering tool designed to analyse the behaviour of beams subjected to different types of loads. It helps users understand how beam geometry, material properties, support conditions and applied loads influence the internal forces, bending behaviour and deformation of a beam. The simulator supports cantilever and simply supported beams, making it useful for engineering students, educators and anyone learning the fundamentals of structural and mechanical analysis.

1. Selecting the Beam Type and Length

The analysis begins by selecting the beam's support configuration. A cantilever beam is fixed at one end and free at the other, whereas a simply supported beam rests on supports at both ends. This selection determines the support conditions used in the calculations. Enter the total beam length in metres to establish the span over which the loads act and the positions at which the results are evaluated.

2. Material Properties

The material selection section defines the mechanical properties used in the analysis. Young's modulus represents the material's stiffness and is used when calculating elastic beam deflection. Yield strength represents the stress at which permanent deformation may begin and is used to assess the calculated bending stress against the selected material's specified strength.

Users can select an available material or enter custom properties where supported. The entered values should correspond to the material being analysed and must use the units specified beside the input fields. Young's modulus must be positive for a meaningful elastic deflection calculation. Changing the material properties can affect the predicted deflection and the calculated factor of safety.

3. Selecting the Cross-Section

The cross-section determines how effectively the beam resists bending. Depending on the available options, users can select shapes such as solid rectangles, solid circles, hollow circles, hollow rectangles, I-sections, T-sections and C-sections. The dimensions required for the selected shape are displayed so that the user can enter the appropriate width, height, thickness, diameter or other geometric measurements.

The simulator uses the selected cross-section and its dimensions to calculate the second moment of area, commonly denoted by I. This geometric property describes the distribution of the cross-sectional area about the relevant bending axis. For a given material and loading condition, a section with a larger second moment of area will generally experience less elastic bending deflection. The result depends on the actual section geometry and the axis about which bending occurs.

4. Applying Loads to the Beam

Loads can be added individually so that different loading arrangements can be examined. Each load has its own input fields, and the position or extent of the load must lie within the beam's length.

  • Point load: Enter the force magnitude in newtons and specify its position along the beam in metres. The load acts at the selected location.
  • Uniformly distributed load (UDL): Enter the load intensity in newtons per metre and specify the starting and ending positions of the loaded region. The simulator uses the load intensity and loaded length to determine the equivalent resultant force for equilibrium calculations.
  • Applied moment: Enter the moment magnitude in newton-metres, choose its position along the beam and select the applicable clockwise or anticlockwise direction. Applied moments contribute to the moment equilibrium of the beam and affect its bending behaviour.

Multiple loads can be combined to study more complex loading arrangements. The magnitude, position, direction and extent of each load influence the resulting support reactions, shear force, bending moment and, where calculated, beam deflection.

5. Support Reactions and Internal Forces

The simulator determines the support reactions from the equilibrium conditions of the selected beam. These calculations use the applied forces and moments, together with their positions, to satisfy the equations of static equilibrium. The resulting reactions describe the forces and, where applicable, the reaction moment developed at the supports to balance the loading.

The shear force diagram (SFD) illustrates how internal shear force varies along the beam, while the bending moment diagram (BMD) shows the variation of internal bending moment. Point loads produce changes in the shear force diagram, uniformly distributed loads produce continuous changes in shear force, and applied moments produce jumps in the bending moment diagram. The exact diagram shape and sign depend on the loading arrangement and the sign convention used by the simulator.

6. Beam Deflection and Bending Stress

Beam deflection describes the displacement of the beam from its original, unloaded position. For a beam analysed within the linear elastic range, deflection is related to the bending moment, Young's modulus and the second moment of area. The flexural rigidity, EI, represents the resistance of the beam to elastic bending. A stiffer material or a cross-section with a larger second moment of area generally results in smaller deflections under the same loading conditions.

Where the deflection calculation is available, the deflection curve provides a visual representation of the beam's displacement along its length. The curve should be interpreted using the displayed scale and units. Deflection calculations based on elastic beam theory assume appropriate beam behaviour and material properties; large deformations, plastic behaviour and other effects outside the implemented model may not be represented.

Bending stress is associated with the internal bending moment and the distance from the neutral axis. For a beam in elastic pure bending, the flexure formula relates bending stress to the bending moment, the distance from the neutral axis and the second moment of area. The calculated stress can be compared with the specified yield strength to help assess the beam under the analysed loading condition. If a factor of safety is displayed, it should be interpreted according to the stress measure and calculation method used by the simulator.

7. Visualisation and Calculation Results

The simulator presents the beam and its loading arrangement visually, helping users identify the support locations, applied loads and their positions along the span. The shear force and bending moment diagrams provide additional information about how the internal forces vary along the beam. Where supported by the current configuration, the results section also displays calculated quantities such as support reactions, maximum shear force, maximum bending moment, bending stress, deflection, second moment of area and factor of safety.

Results are updated in response to changes in the beam dimensions, material properties, cross-section or loading inputs when the relevant calculations are triggered. This allows users to compare different configurations and observe how changing one parameter affects the overall response. Values should always be interpreted alongside their units, the selected support conditions and the assumptions of the analysis.

8. How to Use the Results

For a meaningful analysis, select the correct beam type, enter the beam length, verify the material properties, choose the appropriate cross-section and provide the dimensions required for that section. Add each load with its correct magnitude, position, direction and loaded length where applicable. Check that the inputs are consistent with the units displayed by the tool, then review the support reactions, diagrams and numerical results.

The simulator is intended for educational use, preliminary engineering calculations and exploring the relationship between loads, internal forces, material stiffness, cross-sectional geometry and beam deformation. Its results depend on the correctness of the user inputs and the assumptions built into the implemented calculation methods. They should not be used as the sole basis for the design, certification or safety assessment of a real structure. Practical designs should be verified using appropriate engineering standards and, where necessary, reviewed by a qualified engineer.
